**Modern reproduction.** Fixed equal star span 40/3°, Punarvasu [80°, 280/3°). Angular balance = 16 × (280/3 − λ)/(40/3). For the time method, solve the two boundary crossings by bisection of the sidereal Moon. Entry JD 2410909.285556703, exit 2410910.367660084. The fraction of transit time remaining at birth times 16 gives the second row. The Moon moves at a variable rate. `evidence/replay.py` checks returned Moshier flags and computes both dates, both methods and the complete signs. No tuning. The other civil morning opens in Saturn.

**Historically motivated alternatives.** HPA Appendix A prescribes (AD year − 397) × 50⅓ arcseconds. For 1888 this gives 20°50′47″. The 1912 example actually prints 937 on the subtraction line, while its difference 1,515 and the numbered instruction agree on 397. Chapter IX explicitly allows omission of odd-day precession and teaches linear longitude interpolation between Greenwich mean noons. Applying both operations to modern noons tests the published algorithm separately from the source of its numbers. On this profile the angular residual increases. The fixture also records the experimental passage fractions from the corresponding interpolated longitude functions. Those crossings are synthetic, distinct from a pañcāṅga’s recorded star ingress and egress. [HPA IX, pp. 37–39, scans 53–55, Appendix A pp. 278–279, scans 294–295](https://archive.org/details/hindu-predictive-astrology-b.-v.-raman/page/n294/mode/1up)

**A contemporary numerical check.** The 1888 almanac’s astronomical day runs from noon. Thus 23:36 civil UT falls at 11h36 in its 28 September rows. Scans 173–174 give lunar right ascension and declination. Read 11h and 12h, respectively 6h55m47.06s, +21°31′05.0″ and 6h57m58.86s, +21°30′42.4″. Convert both to ecliptic longitudes, interpolate at 0.6 and subtract the annual offset. The noon variant uses hour 0 and 24 at 11.6/24. The coordinate rotation uses modern true obliquity of date 23.452744° and holds it fixed over the bracket. This is a hybrid comparison, with the historical table values retained in `evidence/almanac.py`. No evidence attributes that book to Raman. [1888 American Ephemeris, pp. 156–157, scans 173–174](https://archive.org/details/americanephemer00docgoog/page/n172/mode/1up)

**What the printed balance implies.** Exact angular inversion gives 82.384259259°, or Gemini 22°23′03.33″. HPA’s Table II gives 13y 2m 12d at Gemini 22°20′. Table III subtracts 22 accounting days for 3′ in Jupiter. At **22°23′**, those two printed entries yield **13y 1m 20d exactly**. This is a reverse diagnostic. At the independently computed 22°24.733′, using the lower whole minute yields 22°24′ and a subtraction of 29 days (13y 1m 13d). Nearest minute gives 22°25′ and a subtraction of 36 days (13y 1m 6d). Neither ordinary convention reaches 22°23′. Rounding the final balance to one day likewise cannot absorb twelve days. Table II is credited to N. C. Lahiri’s *Indian Ephemeris*. This lookup maps a supplied sidereal longitude to a balance and establishes no choice of zodiac zero for the original chart. [HPA pp. 283, 285–286/PDF 299, 301–302](https://archive.org/details/hindu-predictive-astrology-b.-v.-raman/page/n300/mode/1up)

**Alternate witness.** The expanded *Bhāvārtha* text reads Jupiter 5y 5m 23d, 2,757 accounting days below 13y 1m 20d. Under the same angular relation this would require 88.766204° and a Gemini D9 Moon, whereas the *How to Judge* D9 Moon is in Aries. This diagnoses an incompatibility across records. The early *Bhāvārtha* rāśi plate supplies no starting balance. Withhold a copy-error assignment pending a page image and a worksheet.
